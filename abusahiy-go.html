AbuSahiy Go: from stalled to production in two months

A cross-border logistics platform had been through an internal team and an outsourced vendor and still had no release. I took it over in October 2025. It went live in December and now serves 1,000+ business clients with 4,000+ orders a month.

Role
Project manager
Team
5: backend, mobile, QA, design
Stack
React, Python/Django, PostgreSQL
Period
October 2025 to present

The situation

AbuSahiy Go delivers orders from China, the US, the UAE and Turkey to Uzbekistan. The product has three parts: a client mobile app with live tracking, an admin panel for operations, and a warehouse management system. When I joined, two codebases existed, one from the internal team and one from the vendor, and neither was in production. Management wanted a date.

Decision one: which codebase

The obvious move was a clean rewrite. We audited both codebases with the engineers instead: architecture, how much of the core flows already worked, how hard each would be to maintain. The vendor's code was further along and better structured, so we built on it. That decision alone saved months of development.

Decision two: what ships first

The backlog described a finished product, not a first release. I cut it down to a launchable MVP: the order and tracking flows, warehouse intake, and the admin views operations needed on day one. Everything else moved to later phases, and management agreed to the phased plan before we started building.

How we delivered

I introduced Scrum with short sprints, a groomed backlog, and delivery KPIs the whole team could see. Predictability improved by about 25% and rework fell by 30 to 40%. The first production release shipped in December 2025, two months after takeover, and releases have been regular since.

Product work after launch

With the platform live, I mapped the customer journey for business clients and used it to reorder the roadmap. Key flows were redesigned where the map showed friction, and feature priority now follows what moves orders and retention rather than what was requested loudest.

What I took from it

A rewrite feels safe and usually isn't. Reading the code you already have, together with the people who will maintain it, is the cheapest decision a project manager can make. And a first release is a scope decision, not a feature list.
